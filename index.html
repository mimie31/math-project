📖 The Bioscience Scenario

In intensive finfish aquaculture production (e.g., genetically improved Red Tilapia strains), growth is non-linear and conforms to a sigmoidal (S-shaped) logistic curve. Understanding the instantaneous rate of change—the First Derivative \(f'(x)\)—is critical.

The derivative represents the daily average weight gain (grams/day). In animal husbandry, identifying when the growth rate peaks is vital. Once the derivative peaks and begins to decline, the fish's Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) decreases.

Feeding the stock past this point increases production expenses rapidly, as commercial aquaculture feeds represent nearly 60% to 70% of total operating costs in Malaysia.

Deep-Dive: How Calculus Solves Aquaculture Economics

1 The Biological Peak

The peak of the derivative curve \(f'(x)\) occurs at the inflection point of the cumulative curve \(f(x)\). Mathematically, this is where the second derivative equals zero: \(f''(x) = 0\). Practically, this marks the maximum possible growth efficiency of the fish. After this day, the rate of weight accumulation slows down, and daily energy intake goes more toward body maintenance than tissue growth.

2 The Water Quality Equation

In tropical Malaysia, water temperatures hover near 30°C. In the simulator, the Water Quality index (\(\eta\)) represents this structural microclimate parameter. In an under-aerated system (\(\eta \approx 0.75\)), fish suffer respiratory stress, slowing metabolic enzyme processes, shifting the inflection point to the right, and depressing the peak derivative value.

3 Deciding the Harvest Date

Tilapia are usually harvested in Malaysia between days 90 and 120. By cross-referencing feed cost per day (which is linear-increasing) against cumulative weight value (which is sigmoidal), farmers use the derivative curve to locate the point where profit margins are maximized. Harvesting early targets specific local retail sizing, while day 120 caters to premium frozen processing.
